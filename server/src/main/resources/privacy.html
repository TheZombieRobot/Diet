Moshpoint privacy policy

Last updated: 19 November 2026. Moshpoint is made by the developer named on its Google Play page. Questions about this policy or your information: INSERTCONTACTEMAILHERE.

Moshpoint helps you find your friends at gigs and other crowded places. It was built so that it can do this without knowing who you are or where you are. This page explains exactly what happens to your information.

The short version

  • No account, no phone number, no email, no contacts upload. Nothing to sign up for.
  • You're only findable while you switch on "Go live". You can switch it off at any time, from the app, the notification or the Quick Settings tile.
  • Your location never leaves your phone in a form anyone else can read except the friends and crew you've added.
  • What goes through our server is encrypted on your phone first: we can't read your locations, messages or photos. The one exception is a report you choose to send us.
  • We don't sell, share or use your data for advertising. There are no ads, trackers or analytics in the app, and it doesn't use your phone's advertising ID.
  • Moshpoint is for people aged 18 and over.

What's on your phone

Stored only in the app's private storage on your device, never backed up to the cloud:

  • your display name, your optional photo, and photos of friends (the ones they shared, or ones you picked for them),
  • your friends list, your crews (each ends by itself the morning after its gig) and their chat,
  • a random secret key that lets your friends recognise your phone,
  • places you saved on the map, and spots mates texted you (see "Text my spot"),
  • the photos and clips of your gigs (see "Your gigs"), and the people you've blocked.

Deleting the app deletes all of it.

Earlier test versions had a "Need help" button that could text an emergency contact, and kept that contact's name and phone number on your phone for it. That feature has been removed: the app deletes a contact saved that way the first time it opens after updating, and never sends a text itself.

Text my spot (only if you switch it on)

Text my spot (off until you switch it on in Settings) opens your own texting app with your location already written in: a map link and the coordinates. You choose who gets it and press Send yourself. Moshpoint doesn't see the text or who it goes to, keeps no copy, and needs no SMS permission. The link carries your first name and your spot after a #, which browsers never send anywhere: our server only ever sees that someone opened the page, never where.

When a mate's link opens in your Moshpoint, the spot (and their first name) is read on your phone and kept there as a saved place until you remove it (at most 20 are kept); nothing is sent anywhere, and the finder's arrow to it uses only your phone's own GPS and compass. So that these links open in the app, Android checks a small file on our server when Moshpoint is installed; that check carries nothing about you.

Satellite (only if you switch it on)

"Use satellite when there's no signal" (off until you switch it on in Settings) lets crew messages, your shared location and reading the crew chat go over your phone's satellite connection when there's no other network. They are the same end-to-end encrypted messages that otherwise go over mobile data, to the same server, at most every two minutes; photos, clips and maps never do. Your carrier carries them and may charge for it. With the switch off, the app sends nothing over a satellite connection.

What goes to your watch (only if you install the watch app)

If you put Moshpoint on a Wear OS watch that is paired with your phone, your phone sends the watch what its screen shows and nothing else: your crew's names and colours, small copies of their photos, how far away each one is and, while you're finding someone, which way they are.

It travels over Wear OS's own link between your phone and your watch: Bluetooth when they're together, and Google's Wear OS sync when they aren't. The watch never gets your crew's keys or anyone's map position, and it never talks to our server. Nothing is sent unless a watch with Moshpoint on it is paired with your phone. Removing the watch app removes its copy.

What goes over the air (Bluetooth, Wi-Fi Aware, UWB)

While you're live, your phone broadcasts a random code that changes every 5 minutes. Only friends who hold your secret key can tell the code is you; to everyone else it's meaningless and can't be linked from one 5-minute window to the next. No name, phone number or device identifier is broadcast.

Phones that are looking for friends broadcast the same kind of rotating code, so that friends' phones can speed up and set up precise UWB ranging.

Upstairs / downstairs: if your phone has an air-pressure sensor, the broadcast also carries its reading, scrambled so that only your friends can read it, so a friend's phone can tell whether you're on the floor above or below them. Air pressure isn't a location: it can't say where you are, only how high you are compared with a friend who was standing next to you earlier. Phones without the sensor send nothing.

Crowd relay: when there's no mobile data, your phone may pass on other Moshpoint users' sealed locations over Bluetooth, and theirs may pass on yours. These are encrypted so that only that person's friends can open them. A phone that relays one can't read it and can't tell whose it is.

Messages without signal: while you're live and there's no internet, the messages you send your crew (chat, "On my way", the meet-up pin) are also passed between crew phones nearby over Bluetooth. The first crew phone that gets signal back sends them on for everyone. These are the same encrypted, signed messages that would have gone to the relay: only your crew can open them, and a phone can't change one or pretend to be you. Chat only travels between your own crew's phones. Quick messages and the pin can also pass through a few other Moshpoint phones, like the crowd relay: a phone passing one on can't read it or tell which crew or person it belongs to. Someone listening learns that a message of a certain size was sent at a certain time, and nothing else. Someone removed from a crew can't read or send these once the crew has its new key, and phones stop using the old key over Bluetooth as soon as they hear it has been replaced.

Who can remove people: only the person who made the crew. Anyone can leave. The crew's key is a fingerprint of its creator's key, so an invite passed on by someone else can't name a different creator, and a removed member can't take the crew over. When the creator leaves, they can hand the crew to another member. (Crews made before this rule keep the old one, where anyone in them could remove people, until they end.)

Crew introductions: phones in a crew also broadcast a short encrypted introduction, so crew members' phones recognise each other. Only the crew can open it. Earlier versions left 4 bytes in it that stayed the same all night. This version only sends a form with nothing constant in it.

What goes to our server

Our server (the relay) passes encrypted messages between phones. It has no accounts and can't tell who anyone is: everything below is sealed on your phone before it's sent, filed under codes that change every few minutes or every day, and deleted when it expires.

Your location: when you're live and have mobile data, your phone uploads your GPS position and battery level (so mates know when your phone is about to die), encrypted on your phone with a key only your friends have, filed under your current rotating code. Friends' phones fetch and decrypt it. The server:

  • only ever sees rotating codes and encrypted bytes,
  • can't read locations and can't tell who anyone is,
  • deletes locations after 15 minutes,
  • doesn't log request contents, codes or internet (IP) addresses. To stop abuse it keeps short-lived counters of how many requests come from each internet address and install code (below), in memory only, and forgets them when they go quiet.

Gig-group introductions (your finding key and name, encrypted with the group's key) may also be posted there briefly so group members can find each other.

Your install code: the first time the app talks to our server, the server gives it a random code (random bytes and a time, signed by the server) that the app sends with its requests, so your phone gets its own fair share of the server instead of sharing one with everyone at the venue. It says nothing about you, the server doesn't store it, and the app only ever sends it to the server that issued it.

Your floor: the air-pressure reading described above is uploaded the same way as your location (encrypted on your phone for your friends only, filed under a rotating code, deleted after 15 minutes), so friends out of Bluetooth range can see whether you're upstairs. Only builds with our own server do this, and not while you're in ghost mode.

Which relay: a build without our own server (such as a test build) uses the free public relay ntfy.sh for this. It gets exactly the same thing: encrypted bytes filed under a code that changes every 5 minutes. It can't read them or tell who you are, but like any website it sees your phone's internet address when it connects. ntfy.sh keeps messages for up to 12 hours (see its own privacy policy).

Crew chat, pings, meet-up pins and moments (optional): messages you send your crew (chat, "On my way", "Where are you?"), a meet-up pin you drop, and photos and short clips (with their sound) you choose to share as moments (with where you took them) are encrypted on your phone with your crew's key, so only people who scanned your crew's QR can open them. They pass through the same relay under codes that change daily or every 5 minutes. Our relay deletes pings and pins after 15 minutes, moments after 24 hours, and crew chat after 72 hours (3 days); the public relay deletes everything within 12 hours. Nothing is shared unless you tap to send it.

"I'm here" (optional): when you pin your spot with I'm here, the spot you chose, the air-pressure reading there (for upstairs/downstairs) and the time go to your crew the same way as a meet-up pin: encrypted with your crew's key and signed by you, through the relay and, with no signal, Bluetooth. Your crew's phones show it for 20 minutes at most, or until you walk away or take it down. People following your crew from home aren't shown it. While your pin stands and the app is open or you're live, your phone checks your GPS, footsteps (motion sensor) and air pressure, on the phone only, to take it down when you leave.

Private messages (optional): a message you send one crew mate with Message Sam is encrypted on your phone so that only that person's phone can open it: not the rest of your crew, not our server, not the public relay and not any phone that passes it on. Your name and your signature are inside the encrypted part, so nobody but them can tell who sent it or who it is for, and every private message is the same size. It goes through the same relay as crew chat (in a mailbox shared by the whole crew, kept for up to 72 hours, 12 hours on the public relay) and, with no signal, through your crew's phones nearby over Bluetooth, never strangers' phones. The relay can see that your crew sent a private message, when, and the internet address it came from, but not who it was for. The conversation is kept on your two phones only and is deleted when the crew ends. Muting someone happens on your phone only; they aren't told. People following your crew from home can't send or receive private messages.

Your photo (optional): if you set one, it's encrypted on your phone with a key only your friends have and stored under an id that changes weekly, for up to 8 days, so friends' phones can show your face. The server can't see it. Removing your photo shares an empty one, so friends' copies are cleared the next time they refresh.

Your gigs (on your phone only): photos and clips your crew shares are kept on your phone, in the app's private storage, so you can look back at the night. They never leave your phone from there. You can delete a gig any time, uninstalling removes them all, and "Save to phone" copies them into your own gallery only when you ask.

Adding your own photos from that night (optional): Android's photo picker shows your photos; only the ones you pick are copied into the gig, on your phone. Moshpoint has no access to the rest of your photos and never looks through them.

Finding gigs and venues

Smart search and "Coming up near you": when you plan a gig, the app can suggest acts and shows as you type, and the home screen can list shows coming up near you. To ask, your phone sends our server what you've typed (from the first letter), your phone's country (from the mobile network, the SIM or your language setting) and at most a region: in New Zealand, the island, worked out on your phone; elsewhere, a region from the server's own list, picked on your phone. Never your position, and nothing about you or your crew. The server answers from lists of shows it builds itself from Ticketmaster, SeatGeek and Bandsintown, so nothing you type goes to them. These are Moshpoint+ features: until Moshpoint+ is on sale, our server says no and the app hides them for a day, so you type your gig in by hand.

Finding a venue you typed (optional): if you type a gig in with a venue, the venue's name is looked up once, when you start the crew, so the crew's map knows where it is. (Joining a crew whose venue has a name but no position does the same, once.) The name and a very rough area (a box about 300 km across, not your position) go to our server, which asks OpenStreetMap's search service, Nominatim. Test builds without their own server ask Nominatim directly. Nothing about you or your crew is sent with it. Leave the venue blank and nothing is sent.

Maps: the street map comes from OpenFreeMap, drawn from OpenStreetMap's data. Your phone downloads the map for the area you look at and, when you create or join a crew, for 2 km around the venue. That copy is kept on your phone so the map still works with no signal, and is deleted a few days after the gig. Like any website, OpenFreeMap sees your phone's internet address and which area of the map was asked for. Nothing about you, your friends or your crew is sent.

Reporting and blocking

Blocking someone hides their crew chat messages, photos, clips and private messages on your phone, and their new private messages are dropped when they arrive. It happens only on your phone: nothing is sent, they aren't told, and they stay in the crew. Your blocked list (their name, the crew's name and their crew key) stays on your phone; Settings → Blocked people shows it and unblocks.

Reporting something sends a copy of it to us, because we can't see your crew's messages otherwise: they're end-to-end encrypted. Before anything is sent, the report screen lists what goes:

  • the message's text, or the photo (made smaller) or four still frames of the clip (made smaller), with its caption;
  • the name and crew key of the person who posted it (their identity in that crew only);
  • a code for the crew (a hash of its id: not its name, invite or key) and when it was posted;
  • the reason you picked, your note if you wrote one, and the app's version.

Nothing about you goes with it: not your name, your key, your phone's address or an install id. The person you report isn't told who reported them. Reports are stored on our server and kept for 90 days, then deleted; only the app's developer can read them, with a secret key, to deal with them. A report about a serious crime may be passed to the police.

Before your first crew chat message, photo, clip or private message, the app shows the community rules once and asks you to agree.

Continue with Facebook (optional, only in versions that offer it)

Nothing reaches Meta unless you tap "Continue with Facebook": Facebook's login library in the app only starts then, and versions of the app without a Facebook login don't show the button. When you do, Meta's library talks to Meta to log you in (Meta's own privacy policy covers what it receives, such as your Facebook session and basic information about the app and your phone), and Facebook tells the app your name and profile photo (nothing else: no friends list, email or posts). Your first name fills in your Moshpoint name and the photo becomes your Moshpoint photo, shared with your friends as described above. The library's analytics, advertising-ID collection and automatic start are switched off. You can stop using it any time; it doesn't post anything on your behalf.

Where our server is, and how long it keeps things

Our relay server runs in Sydney, Australia (on Fly.io). What it holds is encrypted on your phone before it's sent, and deleted when it expires:

WhatKept for
Locations, floor readings, pings and the meet-up pin15 minutes
Moments (photos and clips)24 hours
Crew chat and private messages72 hours (3 days)
Profile photos8 days
Reports (the one thing it can read)90 days

Fly keeps one daily snapshot of the server's storage for one day, so a copy of that encrypted data can outlive its deletion by up to a day; it's as unreadable there as on the server. Map tiles come from OpenFreeMap, venue lookups from OpenStreetMap's Nominatim, and the show lists for smart search from Ticketmaster, Bandsintown and SeatGeek (asked by our server, never with anything about you), each under its own privacy policy. Builds without our server (such as test builds) use the public relay ntfy.sh instead. If you have a Wear OS watch, the phone-to-watch link is Google's (see "What goes to your watch").

Deleting your data

There's no account to delete. Delete the app: everything on your phone is gone. Anything on our server (encrypted locations, messages, moments and photos) expires on its own within 8 days, and a report within 90 days. To ask about a report, or anything else on this page, write to INSERTCONTACTEMAILHERE. If you used Facebook login you can also remove Moshpoint at facebook.com → Settings → Apps and websites.
